### Differences Between Strength vs Hypertrophy Training

Strength and hypertrophy are two primary outcomes we often try to achieve via resistance training. And while they are fairly similar and interrelated outcomes, training can be modified to emphasize one quality over the other. In this video, we will explore what the difference between strength and hypertrophy are, how compatible is it to train for both outcomes, and how to prioritize either strength or hypertrophy in our training routines. First, let's explain what the difference between strength and hypertrophy are. While they are sometimes discussed as almost the same outcome, they are in fact different but related adaptations. To explain what hypertrophy is, let's take a step back and look at muscle growth as a whole. Muscle growth is simply the concept of growing bigger muscles in response to resistance training. And the adaptations that contribute to the phenomenon of muscle growth can collectively be termed hypertrophy. And while hypertrophy is often used as a general term, the primary form of hypertrophy that has been thought to contribute to muscle growth is called in parallel myofibrillar hypertrophy. To explain what this is, let's look at a muscle structure in detail. Essentially, the overall muscle belly is composed of a matrix of contractile tissue. The smallest unit of contractile tissue is called a myofibril. In parallel myofibrillar hypertrophy is essentially just an increase in the number of myofibrils within each muscle fiber. As a result, the muscle fiber increases in diameter. And with each muscle fiber being bigger, the overall muscle belly increases, too. And this is what gives us the visual appearance of having bigger muscles. There are also some other adaptations that have been hypothesized to contribute to muscle growth. These include in series myofibrillar hypertrophy, sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, and hyperplasia. However, it isn't entirely clear how much these adaptations actually contribute to overall muscle growth at this stage. In any case, we can use hypertrophy as a general term that encompasses all of these adaptations collectively. So, in most contexts and for the sake of this video, the term hypertrophy can be used interchangeably with muscle growth. Now that we understand what hypertrophy is, let's explore what strength is. Strength refers to how much weight can be lifted in a specific task. This is usually tested as a one rep max during the classic barbell lifts like the squat, bench press, deadlift, or overhead press. However, strength can also be tested using different exercises, contraction types, and rep ranges. Bodyweight exercises, isometric holds, or max reps with a set weight can all be tests of strength, too. And we can see these differences across different strength sports. Powerlifting is how much weight can be lifted for a single rep in the squat, bench press, and deadlift. Gymnastics requires the strength to move and hold your bodyweight in various ways to perform different skills. And strongman incorporates many different forms of dynamic lifts and isometric holds. There are many adaptations which contribute to strength, but they can all be categorized into two primary categories: structural adaptations and efficiency adaptations. Structural adaptations are those which occur to the physical structure of the muscles and connective tissue, such as muscle growth, changes in pennation angles, and increased tendon stiffness. Efficiency adaptations are those which improve our ability to produce force without changes to the structure of our tissues, such as improved neuromuscular coordination, improved lifting technique, and greater neuromuscular drive. The overall point here is that strength is ultimately a performance outcome. It is a test of our ability to complete a task, which is usually something related to producing high forces. This is different to hypertrophy, which is a structural adaptation, although hypertrophy is one adaptation that can contribute to overall strength performance. So, while strength and hypertrophy are ultimately not the exact same outcome, how compatible are they to train together? Well, strength and hypertrophy seem to be highly compatible and won't really interfere with each other. In fact, almost all forms of resistance training will result in some degree of strength gains and muscle growth simultaneously. And since hypertrophy is one underlying component which contributes to strength, muscle growth seems to be beneficial for strength gains in most cases. And while strength doesn't necessarily have a direct benefit for hypertrophy, it doesn't seem to inhibit muscle growth or be a competing adaptation. So, if you have both strength and hypertrophy goals, you can certainly work towards achieving both simultaneously. However, it should be noted that although strength and hypertrophy don't necessarily have competing adaptations from a physiological perspective, we may run into practical and logistical limitations if you want to maximize both goals simultaneously. Essentially, we all have a finite amount of bandwidth, so to speak, of time, mental energy, and joint tolerance. So, there is only so much time and effort we are able to dedicate to any single goal before running out of one of these limitations. So, if we want to maximize strength or hypertrophy at all costs, then it may leave less time, mental energy, or joint tolerance to be able to complete the training required to maximize the other goal. So, with that background information out of the way, let's now get into the details of how strength and hypertrophy training differ from each other. The first and probably biggest overall difference is the concept of specificity. This is the idea that the amount of transfer our training has to a task is based on how similar our training is to that task. Training that is more similar to the task will improve performance of that task to a greater magnitude, while training that is less similar to the task will improve performance of that task to a lesser magnitude. In simple terms, you get better at what you train. Specificity is probably the most important concept for strength training. Since strength is a performance outcome, we want to train in a similar way to the strength test we want to improve. This relates to exercise selection, rep ranges and load, and technique. For example, if we want to get stronger at a barbell squat for a one rep max, training the barbell squat with heavy loads and low reps will have the most direct transfer to strength performance. An exercise like Smith machine squats will likely have less transfer since the movement is slightly different. Something like leg press will have even less transfer, and leg extensions will have minimal transfer since the movement pattern is even less similar. This was seen in this study, which compared the effects of training with different bench press variations on strength gains. 36 men with at least two years of resistance training experience performed either the Smith machine or dumbbell bench press two times per week for 10 weeks, having their six rep max for each lift tested before and after the training program. It was found that the group training with the Smith machine bench press improved their Smith machine bench press six rep max more than the dumbbell bench group. Whereas the group performing dumbbell bench training improved their dumbbell bench six rep max more than the Smith machine group. Furthermore, specificity also relates to the rules and regulations of the strength test you want to improve. This can influence how we execute the lifting competition and also how we train for strength. For example, according to the IPF, a valid bench press is considered one where the bar touches the chest, it must be paused in that position until a press command is given, pressed until the elbows are fully straightened, feet must be flat on the ground, and buttocks must stay in contact with the bench. But when it comes to hypertrophy, the principle of specificity doesn't really apply. Hypertrophy is not a performance outcome, so there is no real task that we are trying to achieve transfer to. Instead, we are trying to use resistance training as a way to stress our muscles and stimulate growth as a response. This can be achieved using many different exercises, rep ranges, and techniques since there are many ways to induce muscular stress. Moving on to some more specific variables now, let's look at the influence of proximity to failure on strength and hypertrophy outcomes and how it may differ between them. Proximity to failure simply refers to how close to failure we take each set. This is usually gauged via the number of reps in reserve we leave before failure, which can be quantified using the reps in reserve scale. Failure means we could not complete a rep and failed to lift it. Zero reps in reserve means we could not have performed any more reps in that set. One rep in reserve means we could have performed one more rep in that set before reaching failure. Two reps in reserve means we could have performed two more reps before failure, and so on. So, how does proximity to failure influence each outcome? The best evidence we have on this topic is this meta-regression, which looked at the overall relationship between both strength and hypertrophy. It was found that there was an overall positive relationship between training closer to failure and the magnitude of muscle growth achieved. But for 1 RM strength gains, when load is equated, there was no directional relationship at all for proximity to failure and strength gains. So, this would suggest that training close to failure is beneficial for muscle growth, as we would expect. Although, if we take the same weight and train closer or further from failure, it doesn't really seem to influence strength outcomes. And while this may be true in isolation, there are a few reasons you may need to train close to failure to maximize strength gains. First is that as load is increased over time, this makes training closer to failure inevitable at some point. To test your one rep max, that is by definition the maximum weight you can lift for one rep, which is equivalent to zero reps in reserve. And since specificity is highly important for strength gains, eventually we will need to train heavy to maximize transfer to strength performance. And the other reason is because we will probably want to include some hypertrophy training to support long-term strength gains. Since muscle growth is an underlying quality which contributes to strength, hypertrophy of the prime movers can be beneficial for strength progress over time. So even though training closer to failure might not be directly beneficial for strength gains during the competition exercises, it does seem to be beneficial for muscle growth. So it will be more effective to train closer to failure during accessory lifts that have the purpose of growing the prime movers. For some practical recommendations, training closer to failure is recommended to maximize muscle growth. Try to leave no more than around two reps in the tank during each set. For strength gains, we don't really need to focus too much on proximity to failure for the primary lifts. Rather, we should focus more on the load and let proximity to failure naturally fall where it needs to as a result of the load used. Next, let's look at how volume influences strength versus hypertrophy. Volume in this context refers to the total number of sets we perform per week. This is usually defined as the total number of sets for each muscle group per week in the context of hypertrophy training and the total number of sets performed for a particular lift in the context of strength training. So how does the amount of volume we train with influence strength and hypertrophy outcomes? The best evidence we have on this topic is this meta-regression, which aimed to establish the relationship between volume and both outcomes. It was found that training with more volume had a positive effect for both strength and hypertrophy. However, volume had a larger effect for muscle growth with further benefits when training a muscle with more than 30 sets per week. And while there also seems to be a positive effect of volume on strength gains, the diminishing returns occur much sooner with a plateauing effect seen after performing more than around five sets of an individual lift per week. So it seems that training with more volume tends to be beneficial for muscle growth. But when it comes to strength, it doesn't seem to take all that many sets to improve our strength gains in the short-term at least. However, as discussed, for long-term strength gains, we probably want to grow the prime movers over time to improve our ultimate strength potential. So it may be beneficial to train the prime movers of the strength lifts with accessory lifts aimed at growing those muscles. For some practical recommendations, doing more volume seems to be beneficial for muscle growth. For minimum dose gains, training a muscle with around five sets per week should be sufficient to see some degree of growth in most cases. For muscles that you want to see substantial growth in, training with somewhere around 10 to 20 sets is a good general range. And for muscles you want to maximize growth of, you may need to train them with more than 20 sets per week. When it comes to strength gains, training a lift with around five to 10 sets per week seems to be enough to basically maximize our short-term strength gains. Although it should be noted that more technical lifts like the Olympic lifts or certain gymnastics skills, training with more volume may be beneficial. Furthermore, we also probably want to train the prime movers of the strength lifts with a further five to 15 sets per week via accessory lifts to promote further muscle growth for long-term strength development. And the next primary variable that we will discuss is progressive overload. Progressive overload is necessary for continual progress for any adaptation, and this applies to both strength and hypertrophy training. At a basic level, progressive overload simply refers to the concept of making training more challenging over time. If we were to perform the exact same training over time, we would initially see some progress, but then eventually plateau. This is where progressive overload comes in as a way to make training more challenging so that we can adapt to a new stimulus and see further progress. For strength training, progressive overload is in a way one and the same as the outcome goal, which is to lift more weight with a specific lift. So basically, the aim is to increase load of the key lifts over time in whatever way is possible. It doesn't really matter what muscles are trained, what our technique looks like, or how this is achieved. As long as we are still abiding by the rules and regulations of the competition federation or the standards that you set for yourself, then increasing load is by definition an improvement in strength. Although it should be noted that this applies to the strength lifts only. For accessory lifts, we would probably want to follow more of a hypertrophy-oriented progression approach. For hypertrophy training, progressive overload is simply a means to continue stressing our muscles. This is kind of like a chicken and egg scenario. Effective training stimulates muscle growth. Muscle growth then allows us to apply progressive overload, and then progressive overload allows us to continue performing effective training. So progressive overload can be seen as a consequence of muscle growth, not necessarily something we need to actively force to happen. And really, this is something that will kind of be negated by training close to failure anyway. If you take each set close to failure, you will naturally perform more reps over time as muscle growth occurs. And if rep ranges get too high, you can increase the load to stay within your target range. And as long as we are training close to failure, it doesn't really seem to matter if we prioritize rep progression or load progression for muscle growth. This was seen in this study, which compared the effects of progressing via increasing reps or load on muscle growth. 43 men and women with an average of around four years resistance training experience performed a lower body training routine two times per week for eight weeks, taking all sets to failure. Half the subjects increased load over this time once they exceeded their target rep ranges, while the other half kept the same load throughout the training period and aimed to increase reps over time. It was found that both groups experienced similar increases in muscle thickness of both the quadriceps and calves with no notable differences between them. It should also be noted that we may not want to sacrifice lifting technique for the purpose of adding reps and load for hypertrophy training. In some cases, this could slightly inhibit the hypertrophic stimulus to a small magnitude. But more importantly, this can increase joint stress, increasing the likelihood of developing joint pain or irritation. So for some practical recommendations, for strength training, prioritize increasing load on the bar for your main lifts within the approximate one to five rep ranges. But for accessory lifts, use a hypertrophy-oriented progression approach. For hypertrophy training, focus on taking each set close to failure, which naturally takes care of progressive overload for the most part. Add either reps and or load over time as your physiology allows without compromising effective technique. The next difference between strength and hypertrophy training is the reps and load we use. While reps and load are two different variables, they are directly related to each other. Training with heavier loads means we can't perform as many reps, while we can perform more reps when training with lighter loads. So what loading ranges should we train with to maximize each goal? Well, when it comes to strength, training with heavier loads and lower reps are found to be superior. This is likely due to the principle of specificity, where heavier loads are more similar to the task of lifting heavy weights. But when it comes to hypertrophy, there seems to be a much larger range we can train with and achieve equal muscle growth. So long as we take each set fairly close to failure, the exact reps and load used don't seem to matter all that much. This was seen in this meta-analysis, which explored the body of evidence looking at the influence of load on strength and hypertrophy. Loads were categorized as light, defined as anything greater than a 15 rep max, moderate, between a nine to 15 rep max, and heavy, an eight rep max load or heavier. For strength, it was pretty clear that heavy loads were superior to light loads, moderate loads were superior to light loads, and heavy loads were superior to moderate loads. In other words, training with heavier loads is generally better for strength gains. But for hypertrophy, there were no clear benefits when comparing light versus heavy, light versus moderate, or moderate versus heavy loads. So for some practical recommendations, we want to train with heavy loads to maximize strength gains. Train within the approximate one to five rep range with the specific strength lift with challenging weights. For muscle growth, focus more on taking each set close to failure rather than the exact reps and load. For some general recommendations, I would suggest performing most of your hypertrophy training somewhere within the approximate five to 15 rep range. Next, let's look at exercise selection and how it differs between both goals. When it comes to hypertrophy training, the role of exercise selection is simply to work the muscle group you are trying to train. So, the key idea for exercise selection when it comes to hypertrophy is to make sure the exercise involves fatigue of the target muscle to be the limiting factor of each set. In other words, if we were to train to complete failure, fatigue of the target muscle should be the factor which causes failure to be reached. Beyond this, there are a few other factors which may make some exercises slightly more effective in isolation, but aren't nearly as important as this primary consideration. Exercises which take the target muscle into a larger stretch under load seem to be slightly favorable. Exercises which involve more stability of other joints that aren't related to the target muscle may help us to train the target muscle closer to true muscular failure. And it may be beneficial to train some muscles with multiple different exercises rather than a single exercise. With these general principles in mind, we can see that there are different exercises and combinations of exercises that can be effective for muscle growth. So, exercise selection can be quite flexible for muscle growth as long as the exercise allows you to train the target muscle close to failure. However, when it comes to strength gains, specificity is the number one priority. Basically, we want to perform the specific exercises that we want to get stronger at somewhat regularly. This was shown in this meta-analysis which looked at the effects of training with machines versus free weights on strength and hypertrophy outcomes. It was found that there was no significant benefit for either free weights or machines on muscle growth. Whereas for strength, training with machines was better for getting stronger at machine exercises, while training with free weights was better for getting stronger at free weight exercises. And although we want to perform the lifts we want to get stronger at, we may include variations of these exercises at some point as a means to alleviate joint stress and monotony. Generally speaking, when we are further away from the time we want to peak our strength, we can afford to be a little less specific with our exercise selection. This may include variations of the competition lifts, performing them with a different technique than the competition standard, or performing them with different lifting equipment than the competition style. But when we are closer to the time we want to peak, we want to be more specific with exercise selection. This includes performing the competition lifts with competition standard technique using the lifting equipment that will be used in competition. For example, a powerlifter will want to perform competition style barbell squat, bench press, and deadlift in the lead-up to a competition they are peaking for. But if they don't have any competitions for multiple months, they may decide to perform hack squats, dumbbell bench press, and stiff-leg deadlifts as a means to alleviate joint stress and to shift the emphasis more to a hypertrophy focus. We want to lift with the technique that allows us to lift the most weight possible since that is the ultimate goal. This involves using the most mechanically efficient technique that is permitted within the rules of the federation or your own standards. ### Should You Train Strength, Power & Hypertrophy in the Same Week?

Should you train power, strength, and hypertrophy all in the same week, or should you separate them into different blocks of training? This is one of the biggest questions in periodization. If you train power on Monday, strength on Wednesday, and hypertrophy on Friday, we would call that undulating periodization. You're undulating between different stimuli day by day. The benefit is that you keep touching each quality every week. You don't completely stop training power while you build muscle. You don't completely stop training strength while you focus on speed. And this can make a lot of sense for a few specific circumstances. One would be for athletes with a sport like soccer or basketball that requires multiple qualities. Another would be for athletes maintaining multiple qualities throughout a long season. And another good example would be for adult athletes who want general fitness without really peaking any one specific quality. But the downside of undulating periodization is that the training can become less specific. If you're trying to really peak maximum strength, but you're also doing power and hypertrophy in the same week, you may not get as much focused exposure to heavy strength work. A block model is the opposite. You might spend several weeks emphasizing just hypertrophy, and then several weeks emphasizing strength, and then several weeks emphasizing power. You can extend blocks based on your weaknesses, and you can stack blocks strategically towards a peak of one specific quality. This traditional periodization model may be superior for athletes peaking for one specific competition like a high jumper, a thrower, a swimmer, a gymnast, or any other athlete preparing for a highly specific competition. Or two, injured athletes who need dedicated time away from jumping and power training to heal and build specific tendons back up in the off-season. Or even three, for beginner athletes who need a good bit of focused strength work before even worrying about power. The downside though is that the qualities that you're not training can start to decay. So, the real answer here is not that one model is always better. It depends on the athlete, the sport, and the time of year. If you need to maintain multiple qualities, undulating can work well. If you want to build towards one specific adaptation, the traditional model can work well there.
